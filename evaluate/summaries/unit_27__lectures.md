# Lectures

This unit contains four comprehensive lectures from AGEC 528 (Feeding the World Sustainably) course materials, covering agricultural land economics, supply response, cropland expansion, water resources, and sustainability.

## Supply Response at the Extensive Margin (lecture_land_Hertel.pptx)

Tom Hertel examines the land-sparing versus land-sharing debate in agriculture using theoretical frameworks and computational modeling with the SIMPLE (Simplified International Model of Prices, Land use and the Environment) model. Case study: Mato Grosso Brazil cropland expansion with soy, corn (for ethanol and hogs), and Embrapa technologies. Key entities: Xingu Indigenous Reserve, AGEC 528, PNAS publication. Discusses governance mechanisms preventing Brazilian deforestation—monitoring, credit access restrictions, supply-chain exclusion, criminal prosecution, protected areas expansion, beef intensification, and Payment for Ecosystem Services (PES).

Central question: Does agricultural intensification spare land? Borlaug hypothesis (intensification spares land) versus Jevons' paradox (rebound effect increases land use). Theoretical framework shows land-use impacts depend on excess demand elasticity, price elasticity of demand, and comparative yields. Mathematical derivation shows conditions for Jevons' paradox when world markets integrated. SIMPLE model validation 1961-2006 (45-year historical baseline) uses exogenous population, income growth, estimated TFP by region/sector (Fuglie). Counterfactual simulations compare scenarios with/without historical Green Revolution (slower productivity growth in GR regions per Evenson). Findings: Historical GR spared global cropland area; generated growth at intensive margin. Future African Green Revolution prospective simulations 2006-2051 appear marginally land-sparing under segmented markets but not with fully integrated markets. Carbon emissions analysis: Historical GR avoided land-use emissions; African GR with integrated markets increases emissions unambiguously in Africa and worldwide due to low relative emissions efficiency (tons output/tons carbon from conversion per West et al. PNAS). Key concepts: Jevons' paradox, price elasticity, emissions efficiency, market segmentation, technological change mechanisms, comparative advantages, policy implications for climate change.

## Supply Response: Potential for Cropland Expansion (lecture_land_Liu.pdf)

Jing Liu (February 5, 2019) analyzes global patterns and constraints on cropland expansion. Sources: HYDE (History Database of Global Environment) version 3.1, Klein Goldewijk et al.; FAOSTAT. Data 1960-2016: global cropland 1.73 billion hectares; expansion in developing countries, contraction in developed countries. Per-capita arable land declining due to population growth outpacing expansion. Agricultural growth drivers: TFP (Total Factor Productivity, Fuglie 2012), area expansion, intensification. Borlaug vs. Jevons paradox factors: demand elasticity, technological scope (global vs. local). Price elasticity of cropland supply (extensive margin) and crop yield response (intensive margin); technological change shifts production isoquant, intensification moves along isoquant.

Available cropland: 4.5 billion hectares suitable; 1.5 billion hectares in use 2006; 1.2 billion hectares remaining (1.3× USA size). Uncultivated land less productive: 60% of expandable land concentrated in Brazil, USA, Russia, Argentina, Australia, Sudan, China, Congo, Kazakhstan, Angola, Canada, Mozambique, Madagascar. Suitability ≠ profitability: SSA (Sub-Saharan Africa) 400+ million hectares suitable but scarce in profitable land due to remoteness and infrastructure (Liu and Villoria 2016; transportation time critical). Irrigation: 300 million hectares equipped 2006 (doubled since 1960); projected +20 million hectares by 2050 in developing countries = 20% current cultivated land (Alexandratos and Bruinsma 2012). Urbanization encroachment: 271 million hectares (2000) to 621 million hectares (2040); displaces 65 million tons crop production.

Environmental consequences: Land cover change (tropical deforestation) 2nd largest GHG source; commercial agriculture main driver. Carbon loss cropland expansion in tropics nearly 2× temperate regions (West et al. 2008). Habitat conversion, degradation, fragmentation, climate change, harvesting, pollution impacts (Newbold et al. 2015 Nature): worst-affected grid cell 32% reduction local richness. Soil erosion 2.5% increase 2012 vs. 2001 (Borrelli et al. 2017 Nature Communications); least developed economies highest rates.

## Water Quality and Quantity (lecture_water_Bowling.pptx)

Dr. Laura Bowling (Dept. of Agronomy, AGEC 528 lecture, Feb 6, 2018) addresses freshwater sustainability. Water resources defined as availability + infrastructure capacity. Three components: Groundwater (aquifers), Surface water (rivers, reservoirs, lakes), Green water (soil moisture vapor to atmosphere). Renewable water resources: river flow, soil water, accessible aquifer percolation (Falkenmark and Rockström 2006). Scarcity = demand/supply ratio (multi-year average); physically abundant areas can be economically water scarce. Physical vs. Economic scarcity: insufficient resources vs. insufficient investment capacity.

Drought definition: lower-than-normal rainfall (extended period, months-years); natural temporary climate departure; meteorological drought (inadequate rainfall for crops), agricultural drought (insufficient soil moisture), hydrologic drought (lower surface/subsurface supply). Variability differs by region; not all arid regions drought-prone. Crop water productivity (water use efficiency): ratio net benefits to water required ("Crop per Drop"); plant scale carbon assimilated/water transpired; field scale grain yield/water applied; economist value/water applied. Harvest Index HI = grain yield/biomass; higher transpiration increases biomass not necessarily yield.

Water stress index (WSI) at 10-digit HUC (Hydrologic Unit Code); irrigation dominates demand, surface water dominates supply in some regions. Freshwater scarcity 2025 (Scientific American Aug 2008). Environmental costs: Reisner themes (Sabo et al. 2010 PNAS Cadillac Desert): human water stress, dam/reservoir efficacy, salt-load impacts on agricultural revenue, biodiversity/native fish invasion, virtual water transfers. Great American Desert: transition 100th meridian; runoff <10 cm/year. Dam density varies East-West; firm yield (maximum reliably satisfiable demand) decreases 2% (large reservoirs) to 6% (small); 56% of 25 widespread non-native fishes are piscivores adapted to stable regimes. Groundwater development: recharge/extraction imbalances, Ogallala aquifer decline, land subsidence. Aquifer compaction (irreversible porosity loss) from unconsolidated aquifer water removal. Groundwater mining: withdrawal exceeding recharge; recovery takes hundreds-thousands years (USGS Groundwater Depletion 1900-2008).

## Water as Critical Factor in Global Agriculture (lecture_water_Hertel.pptx)

Thomas Hertel outlines water-food nexus, water use efficiency determinants, groundwater irrigation role, SIMPLE-G framework (water+cropland+nitrogen), adaptation strategies, virtual water trade. Irrigation 40% global crop production from 20% area; 70% global freshwater withdrawals (>50% consumption). Most irrigation in Asia; <6% Africa cropland irrigated. Water productivity (kg/m³) in wheat highest moist temperate regions, lowest rainfed dry hot conditions (Zwart et al. 2010). Keller and Seckler mechanisms: increase harvest index, plant density reducing evaporation, transpiration efficiency, temporal/spatial production relocation.

Water balance yield gap SSA: blue water losses (surface runoff limiting infiltration, percolation); potential yield 1→3 t/ha with productive water use (no nutrient deficiency, no evaporative loss); 5 t/ha without deep percolation; 7.5 t/ha with all local rain productive (Rockström et al.). Smallholder agriculture increases yields with less proportionate water increase limiting evaporative losses. Groundwater irrigation growth—natural surface-water scarcity response, accessible without large government initiatives, drought resilient, on-demand reliability. But growth concentrated in arid low-recharge areas; non-renewable groundwater abstraction tripled 1960-2000 (Wada et al. 2012); 30% current withdrawal unsustainable, rising to 40% by 2100. SIMPLE-G framework (Liu et al. 2017): global grid (30 min) + 15 regions, irrigated-rainfed distinction. Adaptations to water scarcity: inter-basin transfers, market integration (virtual water trade), TFP investment. Reducing unsustainable irrigation may increase cropland expansion/GHG emissions; adaptations can ameliorate (Liu et al. ERL 2017): no-adaptation 0.826 GtC, inter-basin transfer 0.053 GtC. Virtual water trade addresses scarcity via agricultural removal from water use; by 2030 1/3 global population will live in river basins where demand exceeds supply by 50% (McKinsey). Irrigation reliability index map (Liu et al. 2014 GEC) shows geographic food-trade restructuring. Biofuel land-water nexus (Taheripour, Hertel, Liu 2013): High Plains aquifer supports bulk irrigated US corn; some aquifers overdrawn (Kansas usable lifetime estimates NRC/NAS 2011). Key findings: irrigated croplands higher yields than rainfed in same Agro-Ecological Zone (AEZ); irrigation constraint increases cropland area requirement; rainfed areas higher carbon emissions factors. Energy Sustainability Society journal (doi:10.1186/2192-0567-3-4).

Australian drought adaptation case 2002/3: Murray-Darling Basin 29% water usage drop, rice 70% drop; water trading enabled shift rice production to other regions, modifying TERM-H2O CGE model predictions. Key concepts: water-food nexus, irrigation vulnerability index, virtual water, AEZ classification, water scarcity metrics, biofuel climate impacts.

## Summarized attachments

- **Supply response at the extensive margin** (lecture_land_Hertel.pptx, file): Tom Hertel's supplementary lecture on the land-sparing versus land-sharing debate in agriculture, featuring case studies of Mato Grosso Brazil cropland expansion and theoretical frameworks for understanding how agricultural intensification affects global land use and deforestation through the SIMPLE model.

- **Supply Response: Potential for Cropland Expansion** (lecture_land_Liu.pdf, file): Jing Liu's lecture (February 5, 2019) analyzing global cropland patterns, expansion constraints, and environmental consequences including tropical deforestation impacts, soil erosion, and biodiversity loss, with data on land suitability versus profitability.

- **Water quality and quantity** (lecture_water_Bowling.pptx, file): Dr. Laura Bowling's lecture on freshwater sustainability covering water resources, scarcity, drought definitions, crop water productivity, and environmental costs including groundwater mining, aquifer compaction, and dam impacts on river systems and biodiversity.

- **Water as a Critical Factor in Global Agriculture and a Determinant of Land Use Expansion** (lecture_water_Hertel.pptx, file): Thomas Hertel's lecture on the water-food nexus examining irrigation's role in agriculture, groundwater depletion trends, water use efficiency mechanisms, and adaptations including inter-basin water transfers and virtual water trade to address future water scarcity impacts on land use and greenhouse gas emissions.
