Global Change and the Challenges of Sustainably Feeding a Growing Planet
Abstract
This chapter begins by reviewing the historical relationship between population and cropland expansion. It also considers the role of per capita income growth in shaping land use—particularly land devoted to non-agricultural uses, including forests, over the historical record. We then turn to the consideration of population as a determinant of future land use reviewing the basic concepts in demography, including the demographic transition, as well as a summary of the latest population projections to 2050. We also consider the interplay between population growth and current differences in consumption patterns across regions. Many of these differences are driven by per capita incomes. As population growth slows down relative to historical growth rates, it is expected that per capita incomes will play a larger role in driving overall food consumption. Therefore we next turn to the role of income as a determinant of consumption behavior. This includes discussions on dietary upgrading—the shift in consumption from starchy foods towards meats, dairy and processed foods—as well as the statistical evidence on how consumers’ responses to food prices and to additional income vary by income level. The chapter concludes with projections to 2050, based on the SIMPLE model, in which we explore the implications of population and income growth rates over this future period on global food demand and land use change.

Featured Researcher
Alexandra Hill,  University of California, Berkeley
Alexandra Hill is an Assistant Professor of Cooperative Extension at UC Berkeley. Her research centers around the US agricultural workforce and seeks to demonstrate how a variety of factors impact worker well-being, quantified in terms of income or health impacts, and how these factors then impact businesses, in terms of key performance indicators like productivity, output quality, profits, and workplace injuries.
Hired farmworkers contribute greatly to US agriculture, however they face a multitude of economic, physical, legal, and emotional hardships in their personal and professional lives. Through her research and outreach, she aims to advance farmworker well-being and contribute to a more resilient and prosperous agri-food system by addressing these and other challenges. In particular, she works to identify avenues to enhance the well-being of agricultural employees while maintaining or improving the economic viability of the businesses employing them.

Modelling Extremes: From Climate Change to Biophysical Impacts and Economic Implications
﻿
Increasing frequency of extreme weather events, induced by rising average temperatures, changing precipitation patterns, and blocked weather systems, are expected to create increasing challenges for agriculture and food production, food security, and resource management in general. Long-term trends and average conditions, in terms of maximum temperatures, length of drought periods, and maximum amounts of rainfall in short periods of time may not hold in the future. In addition, locally confined extreme events alone may not lead to large-scale production failures or socio-economic shocks, but if several events across different regions coincide, substantial aggregate effects may develop. This asks for an improved analytical framework across spatial and temporal scales, in line with the global-to-local-to-global research agenda in GLASSNET.
﻿
Currently available simulation models, ranging from climate to vegetation and crops to socio-economic impacts and adaptation, are still relatively poor in describing the occurrence and related impacts of extreme events, for various reasons. Given this situation, several key questions arise: How can the understanding of extreme weather events and their coincidence be improved? How can the existing modelling chain, from biophysical processes to socio-economic impacts, be expanded? Based on an improved modelling toolset, how are the impacts of extreme weather events likely to propagate through entire global value chains, and to what extent can adaptation options at different scales be better evaluated?
