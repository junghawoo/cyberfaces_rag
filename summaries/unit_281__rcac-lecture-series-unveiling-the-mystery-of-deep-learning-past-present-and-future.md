# RCAC Lecture Series: Unveiling the Mystery of Deep Learning: Past, Present, and Future

A Spring 2025 lecture-series short course hosted by the Rosen Center for Advanced Computing (RCAC) in conjunction with Purdue's Institute for Physical AI (IPAI), led by Dr. Elham Barezi, AI Research Scientist at RCAC. The series traces the historical evolution of deep learning from 1980s neural networks to the 2010s–2020s resurgence, so participants can understand strengths/limitations of techniques over time and choose suitable approaches for their problems and data. Course outline: (1) History and Basics of DNN (traditional ML to DNN); (2) Fundamental deep learning, discriminative to generative (CNN, RNN, Autoencoders, attention; representation learning and feature extraction; VAE, GAN, Diffusion Models); (3) Transformers Era (self-attention, encoders, decoders, masking; transformers for text, image, video, speech); (4) LLMs in Practice (prompt engineering: COT, TOT, Self-Consistency, RAG, Agents; fine-tuning: instruct tuning, RLHF, adapters like LoRA); (5) Deep learning for different domains; (6) AI safety and Governance.

### deep learning series-session 1
*Source file:* `deep learning series-session 1.pdf` — Slides for the first session (March 5th 2025): AI hypes and winters; deep learning from the 1950s; single neurons to deep networks; challenges solved 1950–present (model overfitting, activation function saturation, vanishing/exploding gradient); deep learning weaknesses. Defines AI vs machine learning (supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement) vs deep learning; rule-based/symbolic AI, planning, Genetic Algorithms & Evolutionary Computation. Covers biological neuron structure (cs231n figure), the Perceptron model (Rosenblatt, 1958), Minsky & Papert's 1969 proof that single-layer perceptrons cannot solve non-linearly separable problems like XOR (first AI winter), Minsky's 1982 "The Society of Mind" warning about expert systems and the second AI winter (late 1980s–early 1990s), and reasons for AI winters (hype, economy/funding cuts, weak R&D pipeline). A deep learning history timeline names Aristotle (Associationism, 300 BC), Alexander Bain (Neural Groupings, 1873), McCulloch & Pitts (MCP model, 1943), Donald Hebb (Hebbian Learning Rule, 1949), John McCarthy (Dartmouth Conference, 1956), Frank Rosenblatt (perceptron, 1958), Paul Werbos (backpropagation, 1974), Kunihiko Fukushima (Neocognitron, 1980), Michael I. Jordan (RNN, 1986), Hinton & Rumelhart (backpropagation for MLP, 1986), Yann LeCun (CNNs for handwritten digit recognition, 1989), Hochreiter & Schmidhuber (LSTM, 1997), Nvidia (first GPU, 1999), Geoffrey Hinton (Deep Belief Networks and layer-wise pretraining, 2006; Dropout, 2012), the Transformer (2017), and Vision Transformers (ViTs, 2020). Further slides: neuron components (input, weight, bias, transfer function, activation function); DNN limits (non-convex optimization, SGD theory, expressiveness/universal approximation, interpretability/black-box, data privacy and security); LLM risks (misinformation, hallucination, disinformation; GPT-4 mis-citing a paper link); batch vs stochastic gradient descent, learning rate, optimizers (Adam, RMSprop, AdaGrad); vanishing/exploding gradient causes (saturated sigmoid/tanh activations, poor weight initialization, no batch norm, high learning rate) and solutions (Hinton & Bengio layer-by-layer pretraining 2006 with stacked autoencoders, Xavier & He weight initialization 2010/2015, ReLU 2011 by Glorot & Bengio and the dying ReLU problem, Leaky-ReLU 2013, ELU 2015, Batch Normalization 2015 by Ioffe & Szegedy, Residual Connections/ResNets 2015 by Kaiming He et al., e.g., ResNet-50, ResNet-101); Dropout (2014, Nitish et al.) for overfitting; parameters (weights, biases) vs hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, epochs, optimizer, layers, neurons per layer, activation function, dropout rate, weight initialization, regularization strength). References cite Krishna & Kalluri (IJRTE 2019), Saha/Mitra/Nanjundiah monsoon prediction, Wang et al. LLM model-compression survey (arXiv:2402.09748), Raffel et al. T5 (JMLR 2020), plus towardsdatascience, medium, yunfanj.com ELBO, jeremyjordan.me, Microsoft Research GAN blog, lilianweng.github.io diffusion post, Sebastian Raschka's magazine, Cameron Wolfe substack/tweets, and Cohere top-k/top-p docs.

### deep learning series-session 2
*Source file:* `deep learning series-session 2.pptx.pdf` — Second session (April 18th 2025): fundamental deep learning models from discriminative to generative — the pre-Transformer DNN era (1990–2015): CNN, RNN, attention, and auto-encoders. CNN story: Neocognitron (1980), LeNet-5 (1989–1998, Yann LeCun, MNIST digit recognition), AlexNet (2012 ImageNet winner, GPU-trained), ResNet (2015 residual/skip blocks); ImageNet challenge winners vs earlier SVM plus feature engineering. CNN inspiration from the visual cortex (LGN, V1, V2, V4, IT/PIT/CIT/AIT mapping to conv layers; Yamins and DiCarlo 2016). Convolution mechanics: kernels, weighted averages, smoothing and edge-detector filters, 3D convolution filters, stride, parameter sharing, zero padding, pooling (max, average, L2-norm); key CNN properties (location invariance, local similarity, compositionality, weight sharing) and challenges (no relative-position encoding, limited receptive fields). RNN (1980s/1982, backpropagation through time): hidden state as working memory, parameter efficiency, hard to parallelize; vanishing gradient in RNNs; LSTM (1997) and GRU (2014); attention for machine translation (Bengio et al. 2015 neural machine translation) and image captioning (Xu, Kelvin, et al., arXiv 2015); RNNs as precursors of transformers, GPT, BERT. Pretraining story (~2010): pretraining vs fine-tuning definitions, reduced data requirements, reusable representations (NLP grammar/semantics; vision edges/textures; audio pitch/phonemes), better initialization; layer-by-layer unsupervised pretraining (Hinton 2006, Bengio 2007) with stacked autoencoders; representation learning replacing hand-engineered features (TF-IDF, SIFT, HOG, Wavelet) and end-to-end learning. Generative AI foundations: Bayes rule; discriminative P(y|x) vs generative P(x,y) models (regression, SVM, BERT-family vs Bayes, GDA, GPT family, diffusion); autoencoders for compression; Variational Autoencoders (2013) with ELBO and KL divergence; GANs (2014) generator/discriminator, mode collapse; diffusion models (forward/reverse Markov noise process, variance schedule) behind Dall-E and Stable Diffusion. Same reference list as session 1.
