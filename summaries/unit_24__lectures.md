# Lectures

This unit contains expert-level guest lectures addressing population dynamics, migration, and their interconnections with food systems, resources, and global change.

## Guest Lecture on Population by Brigitte Waldorf

**Context**: AGEC 533, Spring Semester 2019 guest lecture addressing population as fundamental driver of global change.

**Framework**: Integrated systems view connecting population → food demand → resource constraints → social/economic/political/ecological/environmental context → migration patterns

### Population Dynamics

**Population Growth Drivers**:
- Rising population size (2017 UN projections); global demographic growth
- High fertility rates (example: Niger 6.49 children/woman, 2017)
- Young population age structures (example: Niger median age 15.4 years)
- Migration responses to imbalances (labor availability, opportunity disparities)

**Total Fertility Rate (TFR) & Median Age Comparisons (2017)**:
- Niger: TFR 6.49, median age 15.4 (high fertility, young population)
- Mexico: TFR 2.24, median age 28.3
- Bangladesh: TFR 2.17, median age 26.7
- Colombia: TFR 2.00, median age 30.0
- United States: TFR 1.87, median age 38.1
- Canada: TFR 1.60, median age 42.2
- Germany: TFR 1.45, median age 47.1

**Uncertainties**: Population projections subject to fertility volatility, policy changes, unexpected demographic shifts.

### International Migration Patterns

**2017 International Migrant Distribution** (% population in destination):
- Range: >40% to <2% concentrated in global regions
- Top 5 Origin Countries (counts): India, Mexico, Russia, China, Bangladesh
- Top 5 Destination Countries (counts): USA, Saudi Arabia, Germany, Russia, UK

**Migration Categories**:

**Conflict-Induced Migration**:
- Syrian civil war example: ~6.5 million internally displaced; >4 million to Egypt/Iraq/Jordan/Lebanon/Turkey; >1 million to Europe
- Refugee crises creating large-scale population displacement

**Climate Change-Induced Migration**:
- Sea level rise, hurricanes, wildfires, droughts displace populations
- Regional (e.g., New Orleans to Texas post-Katrina) and international (African migrants to Europe) patterns
- Environmental degradation reducing livelihood viability

**Labor Market-Driven Migration**:
- Rural to urban (regional labor market disparities)
- International: brain drain (Caribbean), agricultural workers (Mexico to USA), Polish/Romanian workers to UK
- Skills-based and low-wage labor movement

### Policy Responses

**Restrictive/Exclusionary Approaches**:
- Border walls/barriers
- Bilateral migration control deals (Turkey, Libya agreements)
- Separation/exclusion policies

**Laissez-Faire Approaches**:
- Absence of international migration conventions/frameworks
- Informal tolerance (migrant camps/"The Jungle" in Calais)
- Ad-hoc walk-on tolerance policies

**Proposed Framework**: Policy responses need comprehensive international coordination beyond current piecemeal approaches.

### Spatial Scale Analysis

**Multi-Level Systems**:
- International: Origin-destination country relationships; border/crossing mechanisms
- Regional: Labor market disparities driving migration; housing market constraints
- Local: Family migration decisions; return/onward migration; integration/competition/conflict dynamics

**Push-Pull Mechanisms**:
- Push: Food insecurity, unemployment, climate extremes, conflict/safety concerns
- Pull: Employment opportunities, better living conditions, education, safety

### Climate Change-Migration Nexus

**Causal Chain**: Climate change → extreme weather events → food insecurity/livelihood loss → search for work → migration

**Implications**: Future migration research requires multidisciplinary approach; moving beyond spatial/temporal data truncation; integrating climate, economic, social dimensions.

## Key Concepts

- Total fertility rate (TFR); population age structure
- International migration stocks and flows
- Migration drivers (conflict, climate, labor markets)
- Regional vs. international migration patterns
- Policy frameworks for migration governance
- Climate-migration linkages
- Integration/competition/conflict in destination communities

## Related Dimensions

- Food system sustainability under population growth
- Resource scarcity and migration pressure
- Adaptation strategies (migration vs. local intensification)
- Global governance gaps in migration/refugee systems
- Long-term demographic trends and sustainability

## Learning Outcomes

Understanding population dynamics as fundamental driver of food security, migration, and global change; recognizing complex interconnections between demographics, resources, environment, and human movement; examining policy responses ranging from restrictive to accommodating approaches.

## Summarized attachments
- **Lecture on Population by Brigitte Waldorf** (lecture_population_Waldorf.pdf, file): Guest lecture from AGEC 533 Spring 2019 examining population as global change driver, covering population size/structure, fertility rates, migration patterns (Syria conflict, climate-induced, labor market), international migration policy responses, and research implications for understanding spatial-temporal scales and linkages between local/regional/global migration drivers.
- **Developing optimal agriculture R&D policy** (lecture_TFP_yang_golub_hertel.pptx, file): Presentation by Cai, Golub, and Hertel on optimal agricultural R&D spending under uncertainty considering long lags between R&D and productivity growth, climate change impacts, uncertain population/income drivers, dynamic global land use modeling, total factor productivity functions, and regret-minimization approaches to R&D investment decisions.
- **Yield Growth and Yield Gaps** (lecture_yield_hertel.pptx, file): Presentation by Jeff Volenec from Purdue Agronomy examining historical yield trends for wheat and rice, yield gap analysis between potential and actual farmer yields, biophysical yield potential limits, water use efficiency, nitrogen fertilizer productivity, irrigation depletion (Ogallala Aquifer), and environmental impacts of yield intensification on global food security.
